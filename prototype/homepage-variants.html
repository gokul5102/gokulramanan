Case study · 2026 · Backend lead

How an alert reaches a trader

Traders tell our system what they're watching for, and they expect a ping the moment it shows up. I led the backend for a new alerting service, built from scratch, so those pings arrive quickly even on the busiest days.

About a five-minute read.

What traders needed

A trader might want to know the moment a certain kind of bond comes up for sale, at a certain size. They set that up once and get on with their day. The alert has one job: to arrive while the information still matters.

The service we had did this, but it struggled as more traders set up more alerts. On busy days, the pings came later than they should have.

In trading, a late alert is almost as bad as no alert.

What we built

Market data streams into an alert engine. As each update arrives, the engine checks it against every alert on its own, so one complicated alert can't hold up the rest.

Traders can combine conditions however they like, nesting ANDs and ORs as deep as they need. Behind the main engine sits a backup. The two agree on which one is in charge, and if the main one goes down, the backup takes over without anyone having to step in.

Engineers: this is leader election.
Everyone else: a very reliable backup.

How it went

Once it shipped, alerts reached traders about a third faster than before. The engine also handles 2,000 alerts at once, four times what the old service could manage.

When the desk later added a third type of bond to the platform, its alerts ran on the same engine.

A third faster doesn't sound like much until you're the one waiting.

How I worked

I was the backend lead, so the design and most of the server code were mine. A lot of that code was written with AI agents working alongside me. I'd describe what I wanted, review what came back, and keep the parts that held up.

I now run a monthly session showing colleagues how to work this way.
